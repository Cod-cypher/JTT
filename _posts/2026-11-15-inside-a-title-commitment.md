A title commitment is the document a title company issues before closing. It tells the buyer and lender who owns the property now, what has to happen before the company will insure it, and what the policy will not cover.

It is also the document that takes the most care to type. This guide walks through each part, what belongs in it, and where mistakes most often creep in. Commitment forms and practice vary by state and underwriter, so treat this as a general guide and follow your underwriter's forms.

## What a title commitment is

A commitment is a promise to issue a title insurance policy if certain conditions are met. It is not the policy itself. It is built from two earlier steps:

1. **The search**, which pulls the recorded history of the property: deeds, mortgages, liens, judgments, easements and tax records.
2. **The examination**, which reads that history and decides what affects title today.

The commitment puts the examiner's conclusions into a standard form with three working parts: Schedule A, Schedule B Part I and Schedule B Part II.

## Schedule A: the facts of the deal

Schedule A sets out what is being insured and for whom. It normally includes:

- **The commitment date**, sometimes called the effective date, which is the date through which the records were searched.
- **The policies to be issued**: an owner's policy, a loan policy or both, with the form of each.
- **The proposed insureds**: the buyer for the owner's policy and the lender for the loan policy.
- **The policy amounts**: usually the sale price for the owner's policy and the loan amount for the loan policy.
- **The estate or interest** being insured, most often fee simple.
- **Current vesting**: who holds title as of the commitment date, exactly as the last deed shows it.
- **The legal description** of the land.

Every item here comes from somewhere specific: the order, the contract, the lender's instructions or the search. Schedule A errors are nearly always copying errors, such as a misspelled name, a transposed figure or a legal description that drops a line.

## Schedule B Part I: requirements

Schedule B-I lists what must happen before the policy can be issued. Think of it as the to-do list for closing. Typical requirements include:

- paying off and releasing an existing mortgage;
- recording the deed to the buyer and the new mortgage to the lender;
- paying taxes that are due;
- providing documents that show a company or trust has authority to sign;
- clearing or explaining a judgment or lien found in the search;
- signing affidavits your underwriter requires.

Requirements are where the examination shows. If an open mortgage is missed in the exam, the requirement to pay it off never appears, and nobody at the closing table knows to deal with it.

## Schedule B Part II: exceptions

Schedule B-II lists what the policy will not insure against. Some exceptions are standard and appear on most commitments. Others are specific to the property, such as:

- recorded easements for utilities or access;
- restrictive covenants and subdivision restrictions;
- matters shown on the recorded plat;
- mineral or other reservations in earlier deeds;
- taxes for the current year that are not yet due.

Each specific exception should point to its recorded document, usually by book and page or instrument number, so that anyone reading the commitment can find it.

Most offices keep preferred wording for common exceptions and a preferred order for listing them. Consistency matters, because lenders and attorneys read many of your commitments and notice when one looks different.

## Where commitment typing goes wrong

After years of typing commitments, the same problems come up again and again:

- **Names that do not match the vesting deed.** A middle initial or a "Jr." is not a detail.
- **Legal descriptions retyped instead of compared.** The description should be checked against the deed, the plat and any prior policy.
- **Requirements that do not match the exam.** Every open item in the examination should become a requirement or be explained.
- **Exceptions carried over from a prior file** without checking that they still apply.
- **Recording references with a digit wrong.** One wrong number sends the reader to someone else's document.

None of these are hard to avoid. They are avoided by checking each section against its source, and by having a second person review the commitment before it goes out.

## The commitment and the final policy

The commitment comes before closing and sets the terms. The final policy is issued after closing, once the requirements have been met and the new documents are recorded. Requirements that were satisfied drop away, and the exceptions that remain carry into the policy. A clean, well-organized commitment makes the final policy much faster to prepare.
