"Title and escrow" appears on so many company names and closing documents that it can sound like a single service. It is two jobs. Title is about the property: who owns it, what is recorded against it, and what will be insured. Escrow is about the deal: holding the money and documents until everything the buyer, seller and lender agreed to has happened.

Many offices do both, which is why the words travel together. Knowing where one ends and the other begins makes it easier to see why a file is stuck, who should fix it, and which parts of the work can be handed off.

## In short

- **Title** answers "can this property be sold and insured as agreed?" It runs from the search through the commitment to the final policy.
- **Escrow** answers "has everyone done what they agreed before money and ownership change hands?" It holds funds and documents, then releases them at closing.
- The two meet at the commitment: title lists the requirements, and escrow collects what is needed to satisfy them.
- "Escrow" also has a second meaning: the account a mortgage servicer uses after closing to pay property taxes and insurance.

## What "title" means in a real estate transaction

Title is the legal right to own and use the property. The title side of a transaction checks that the seller can pass clear ownership to the buyer and that the lender's mortgage will be in the position it expects. The work usually runs in this order:

1. **Order entry.** The file is opened with the parties, property, figures and documents from the order.
2. **Search.** The recorded history of the property is pulled: deeds, mortgages, liens, judgments, easements and tax records.
3. **Examination.** Someone reads that history and decides what affects title today: who is vested, what mortgages are still open, which liens and easements matter.
4. **The title commitment.** The examination is turned into a commitment: Schedule A (what is being insured and for whom), Schedule B-I (requirements that must be met before a policy is issued) and Schedule B-II (exceptions the policy will not cover). Our guide [Inside a title commitment](/resources/inside-a-title-commitment/) walks through each part.
5. **Title insurance.** After closing and recording, the final owner's and lender's policies are issued.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau describes the two policies simply: owner's title insurance "protects the homeowner if someone sues" over a prior claim against the home, and most lenders require a lender's policy, "which protects the amount they lend."

## What "escrow" means at closing

In the closing sense, escrow is a neutral holding arrangement. An escrow officer or settlement agent holds the buyer's deposit, the loan funds, the signed documents and the payoff money, and follows written instructions from the parties. Nothing is released until every condition is met. Then the money goes where it should, the deed and mortgage go for recording, and the seller's old loans are paid off.

Typical escrow tasks include:

- opening escrow and receiving the earnest money deposit;
- preparing or reviewing the settlement figures with the lender;
- ordering payoff statements for existing mortgages and liens;
- collecting signed documents and the buyer's funds;
- scheduling and conducting the signing;
- disbursing funds and sending documents for recording.

Who does this varies by state and by office. In some places independent escrow companies handle it, in others the title company does both, and in others an attorney conducts the closing. The jobs are the same; the names on the door differ.

## The other meaning of escrow

People also say "escrow" about something that happens after closing. The CFPB describes an escrow or impound account as one "set up by your mortgage lender to pay certain property-related expenses," usually property taxes and homeowners insurance, funded by part of the monthly mortgage payment.

That account is run by the loan servicer, not by the title or escrow company that closed the deal. When a buyer asks about "escrow" weeks after closing, this is often what they mean.

## Title vs. escrow at a glance

| | Title | Escrow (at closing) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main question | Can this property be sold and insured as agreed? | Has everyone done what they agreed? |
| Works with | Recorded documents, the search, underwriter forms | Money, signed documents, instructions |
| Key output | The commitment, then the final policies | A funded, signed and recorded closing |
| Typical people | Searchers, examiners, title officers, policy typists | Escrow officers, closers, settlement agents |
| Where it ends | When the final policies are issued | When funds are disbursed and documents recorded |

## How title and escrow work together

The two sides hand work back and forth through the whole file:

1. **The order comes in** and both a title file and an escrow file are opened, often as one file in the same software.
2. **Title searches and examines**, then issues the commitment.
3. **Escrow works the requirements.** If Schedule B-I says the existing mortgage must be paid and released, escrow orders the payoff and collects the funds. If it asks for an affidavit or a trust document, escrow gets it signed.
4. **Title updates the file** as requirements are met, and checks for anything recorded since the search.
5. **Closing happens.** Escrow holds the signing, funds the loan and disburses.
6. **Documents are recorded**, and the recording information comes back.
7. **Title issues the final policies**, using the recording data and removing the requirements that were satisfied at closing.

When the two sides work from the same accurate file, closings move. When they do not, the delays are predictable.

## Where closings get delayed

**On the title side:**

- a mortgage that was paid years ago but never released of record;
- a judgment or lien under a name similar to the buyer's or seller's;
- vesting that does not match the contract, such as a missing spouse or an entity name that changed;
- a legal description that does not match across the deed, plat and prior policy;
- a commitment typed with errors, so the wrong requirements reach escrow.

**On the escrow side:**

- payoff statements that arrive late or expire;
- missing signatures, or documents signed in the wrong capacity;
- funds that are not in place on time;
- wiring instructions that must be verified by phone before any money moves.

Many "escrow delays" start on the title side. A requirement that was missed or typed wrong in the commitment surfaces days later as a problem at the closing table.

## What stays in-house, and what can be handed off

Escrow work involves client money, signings and disbursement. That work stays with the title company, attorney or escrow office that is responsible for the closing.

The title production side is different. Opening orders, preparing search packages, examining title, typing commitments and preparing final policies follow written procedures, happen inside the office's own software, and are re-checked before anything is issued. That makes them practical to hand to an outside production team when volume rises.
