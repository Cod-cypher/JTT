Every title file starts with order entry. Someone takes a contract, a lender request or an email and turns it into a file in the production system. It looks like the simplest step in the process, and it is the one that quietly decides how smoothly everything after it runs.

A name keyed wrong at order entry shows up on the commitment, the closing documents and the policy. A missing lender contact holds up a payoff request a week later. This checklist covers what a clean file opening includes and how to keep it consistent.

## What title order entry is

Order entry is opening a new file in your title production software and recording everything known about the transaction at that point: who the parties are, what property is involved, what is being insured and for how much, and which documents came in with the order.

Done well, it means the searcher, examiner, processor and closer each open the file and find what they need already there.

## The file-opening checklist

### 1. File setup

- File number assigned in your numbering scheme.
- Transaction type set: purchase, refinance, cash sale, construction loan or other.
- Products selected: owner's policy, loan policy or both.
- Your office, branch and assigned staff set.

### 2. Parties

- Buyers or borrowers, with names exactly as they appear on the contract or application.
- Sellers, with names exactly as they appear on the contract.
- Marital status or entity type where your process records it.
- Lender name, loan number and the lender contact for the file.
- Real estate agents on both sides, with phone and email.
- Any attorney, mortgage broker or other party who needs updates.

### 3. Property

- Street address, city, county and state.
- Parcel or tax identification number.
- Legal description, if one came with the order.
- Property type: single family, condominium, vacant land, commercial.

### 4. Figures and dates

- Sale price.
- Loan amount.
- Policy amounts for each policy.
- Contract date and expected closing date.
- Earnest money amount, where your office tracks it.

### 5. Documents

- Purchase contract and any addenda.
- Lender's title request or instructions.
- Prior policy or prior file, if the customer supplied one.
- Each document named to your convention, so the next person can find it.

### 6. Workflow

- Opening tasks started.
- Search ordered or assigned, if that is part of order entry in your office.
- Opening notices or welcome emails sent, where your process calls for them.

### 7. Check

- Names compared against the source document one more time.
- Figures compared against the contract.
- Anything missing or contradictory flagged to the right person, not guessed.

## The order entry errors that cost the most

**Names.** Names typed from memory or from an email signature instead of the contract. Every later document inherits the error.

**The wrong county or parcel.** A property near a county line, or two parcels with similar numbers, can send the search to the wrong place.

**Figures that changed.** An addendum changes the price and the file still shows the original. Policy amounts and premiums then come out wrong.

**Missing contacts.** No lender contact or no agent email means someone has to stop and ask later, usually on a deadline.

**Guessing.** When the order is unclear, the right move is to flag it. A guess that turns out wrong is far more expensive than a question.

## Making order entry consistent

Order entry problems are rarely about skill. They are about consistency. Two people in the same office often open files slightly differently, and the differences cause confusion downstream. Three things help:

1. **Write it down.** A one-page procedure for opening each type of file removes most of the variation.
2. **Use the same naming rules** for files and documents, every time.
3. **Check before releasing.** A two-minute comparison against the source documents catches most errors while they are still cheap.
